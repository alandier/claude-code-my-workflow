## Theme 1: Country vs. Industry Effects on Valuation and Returns

### 1. Heston, S.L. and Rouwenhorst, K.G. (1994). "Does Industrial Structure Explain the Benefits of International Diversification?" *Journal of Financial Economics*, 36(1), 3-27.

**Key finding:** Using a sample of 829 firms across 12 European countries (1978-1992), they decompose stock returns into country and industry factors. Country effects dominate industry effects in explaining cross-sectional return variation. This is the foundational methodological framework: a dummy-variable decomposition that can be applied to valuation multiples rather than returns.

### 2. Griffin, J.M. and Karolyi, G.A. (1998). "Another Look at the Role of the Industrial Structure of Markets for International Diversification Strategies." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 50(3), 351-373.

**Key finding:** Extends Heston-Rouwenhorst using a broader sample including non-European markets. Confirms that country factors dominate industry factors for most countries, though the relative importance of industry factors increases for certain sectors (e.g., oil). Relevant as it validates the decomposition approach on a global sample.

### 3. Brooks, R. and Del Negro, M. (2004). "The Rise in Comovement across National Stock Markets: Market Integration or IT Bubble?" *Journal of Empirical Finance*, 11(5), 659-680.

**Key finding:** Documents that the relative importance of industry vs. country factors shifted significantly around 1999-2000, with industry factors temporarily dominating during the tech bubble. After the bubble burst, country factors regained importance. Relevant for understanding time-variation in the country-vs-industry decomposition of valuations.

### 4. Bekaert, G., Hodrick, R.J., and Zhang, X. (2009). "International Stock Return Comovements." *Journal of Finance*, 64(6), 2591-2626.

**Key finding:** Proposes an asset-pricing-based decomposition of international return comovements into cash flow and discount rate components. Finds that country factors remain important even after controlling for global and industry factors, suggesting country-specific discount rates and cash flow dynamics matter for valuation levels.

## Theme 2: Determinants of Cross-Country M/B Differences (Legal Origin, Governance, Financial Development)

### 5. La Porta, R., Lopez-de-Silanes, F., Shleifer, A., and Vishny, R.W. (2002). "Investor Protection and Corporate Valuation." *Journal of Finance*, 57(3), 1147-1170.

**Key finding:** The single most directly relevant paper. Using a sample of 539 large firms from 27 countries, they show that firms in countries with better investor protection (measured by anti-director rights and legal origin) have higher Tobin's Q. Common-law countries have significantly higher valuations. The effect operates both through better growth opportunities and lower expropriation risk. They find a valuation premium of roughly 0.25 in Q for moving from French civil-law to common-law origin.

### 6. La Porta, R., Lopez-de-Silanes, F., Shleifer, A., and Vishny, R.W. (1997). "Legal Determinants of External Finance." *Journal of Finance*, 52(3), 1131-1150.

**Key finding:** Establishes the foundational legal-origins framework. Countries with French civil-law origin have the weakest investor protections, while common-law countries have the strongest. These legal differences explain cross-country variation in the size and breadth of capital markets, which in turn affects firm valuations through cost of capital and access to external finance.

### 7. La Porta, R., Lopez-de-Silanes, F., Shleifer, A., and Vishny, R.W. (1998). "Law and Finance." *Journal of Political Economy*, 106(6), 1113-1155.

**Key finding:** Provides detailed measures of investor protection across 49 countries (anti-director rights, creditor rights, rule of law, etc.). Documents that legal origin systematically predicts the quality of investor protection, which in turn predicts financial development. These institutional variables are the standard controls in any cross-country valuation regression.

### 8. Rajan, R.G. and Zingales, L. (1998). "Financial Dependence and Growth." *American Economic Review*, 88(3), 559-586.

**Key finding:** Shows that industries more dependent on external finance grow faster in countries with more developed financial markets. The mechanism runs through cheaper external finance and better capital allocation, both of which should be reflected in higher valuation multiples for financially dependent industries in financially developed countries. Provides a difference-in-differences identification strategy applicable to valuation analysis.

### 9. Djankov, S., La Porta, R., Lopez-de-Silanes, F., and Shleifer, A. (2008). "The Law and Economics of Self-Dealing." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 88(3), 430-465.

**Key finding:** Introduces the anti-self-dealing index as a refined measure of minority investor protection, replacing the cruder anti-director rights index. Shows this measure is a stronger predictor of stock market development and, by extension, valuations. The anti-self-dealing index has become the preferred institutional variable in the post-2008 literature.

## Theme 3: Evolution of Country Effects and Globalization

### 10. Stulz, R.M. (2005). "The Limits of Financial Globalization." *Journal of Finance*, 60(4), 1595-1638. [Presidential Address]

**Key finding:** Despite globalization, the "twin agency problems" (state expropriation and insider self-dealing) prevent full convergence of valuations across countries. Countries with weaker governance maintain persistent valuation discounts because globalization cannot fully substitute for domestic institutions. Predicts that country effects in valuations should persist even as capital markets integrate.

### 11. Bekaert, G., Harvey, C.R., Lundblad, C., and Siegel, S. (2011). "What Segments Equity Markets?" *Review of Financial Studies*, 24(12), 3841-3890.

**Key finding:** Develops valuation-based measures of market segmentation using industry-relative price-earnings ratios across countries. Finds that market segmentation has decreased over time but remains substantial, especially for emerging markets. Political risk, investment restrictions, and market development explain cross-country differences in valuation ratios even after controlling for industry composition.

### 12. Doidge, C., Karolyi, G.A., and Stulz, R.M. (2004). "Why Are Foreign Firms Listed in the U.S. Worth More?" *Journal of Financial Economics*, 71(2), 205-238.

**Key finding:** Foreign firms cross-listed in the U.S. (via ADRs) have Tobin's Q approximately 16.5% higher than non-cross-listed peers from the same country. The premium is larger for firms from countries with weaker investor protection, consistent with the "bonding hypothesis" -- cross-listing commits firms to higher disclosure and governance standards, which raises valuations. Demonstrates that country-level governance effects on valuation can be partially overcome through institutional choices.

## Theme 4: Japan Discount and Country-Specific Valuation Puzzles

### 13. French, K.R. and Poterba, J.M. (1991). "Were Japanese Stock Prices Too High?" *Journal of Financial Economics*, 29(2), 337-363.

**Key finding:** Analyzes the high P/E ratios of Japanese stocks in the late 1980s. Shows that after accounting for differences in accounting conventions (particularly the treatment of earnings from unconsolidated subsidiaries and inventory accounting), the Japan premium in P/E ratios shrinks substantially but does not disappear. Highlights the critical role of accounting comparability in cross-country valuation studies.

### 14. Rajan, R.G. and Zingales, L. (1995). "What Do We Know about Capital Structure? Some Evidence from International Data." *Journal of Finance*, 50(5), 1421-1460.

**Key finding:** Examines leverage and valuation determinants across G-7 countries. Finds that the same firm-level factors (market-to-book, size, profitability, tangibility) explain capital structure across countries, but country-level institutional features create significant baseline differences. Market-to-book ratios vary systematically across countries even after controlling for firm fundamentals, pointing to country-specific discount rates or growth expectations.

## Theme 5: ROA, Profitability, and Fundamentals Across Countries

### 15. Fama, E.F. and French, K.R. (1998). "Value versus Growth: The International Evidence." *Journal of Finance*, 53(6), 1975-1999.

**Key finding:** Documents that the value premium (high book-to-market firms earning higher returns than low book-to-market firms) exists in 12 of 13 major international markets over 1975-1995. This means cross-country differences in average B/M ratios reflect both differences in expected returns and differences in profitability/growth, both of which vary by country.

### 16. Hou, K., Karolyi, G.A., and Kho, B.-C. (2011). "What Factors Drive Global Stock Returns?" *Review of Financial Studies*, 24(8), 2527-2574.

**Key finding:** Constructs global factor models and shows that a profitability factor (based on cash flow-to-price) has significant explanatory power for international stock returns. Country-level differences in aggregate profitability (ROA) contribute to cross-country variation in valuation multiples. Firms in countries with higher aggregate ROA tend to have higher market-to-book ratios.

## Additional Highly Relevant Papers

### 17. Doidge, C., Karolyi, G.A., and Stulz, R.M. (2007). "Why Do Countries Matter So Much for Corporate Governance?" *Journal of Financial Economics*, 86(1), 1-39.

**Key finding:** Country characteristics explain far more variation in firm-level governance than firm-level variables. Since governance affects valuation (through cost of capital and cash flow tunneling), this implies that country effects in valuations are largely driven by country-level institutional features rather than firm-level governance choices. The R-squared from country dummies alone is very high.

### 18. Bartram, S.M. and Grinblatt, M. (2018). "Agnostic Fundamental Analysis Works." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 128(1), 125-147. [VERIFY exact pages]

**Key finding:** Builds a model predicting market-to-book ratios from accounting fundamentals (ROE, leverage, growth, payout) across 36 countries. Shows that a large fraction of cross-country M/B variation can be explained by cross-country differences in accounting fundamentals, particularly profitability. However, significant country-level residuals remain after controlling for fundamentals, pointing to discount-rate or institutional channels.

## Positioning of Our Paper

The existing literature has mostly studied:
- Country vs. industry in **returns** (Heston-Rouwenhorst and followers)
- The **determinants** of cross-country Q using LLSV-style regressions
- Individual country puzzles (Japan discount)

**Gap we fill:** A systematic Heston-Rouwenhorst-style decomposition applied to **valuation multiples** (M/B, P/E) rather than returns, using comprehensive Compustat Global + North America data. We can:
1. Quantify country vs. industry effects in valuation levels (not returns)
2. Track their evolution over three decades
3. Decompose how much of the country effect is explained by country-level differences in fundamentals (ROA, leverage, growth) vs. residual institutional/discount-rate factors
4. Provide a unified framework that nests the LLSV approach within a variance-decomposition structure

This is distinct from the existing literature because most cross-country valuation papers use relatively small samples of large firms and focus on explaining Q with country-level institutional variables, rather than providing a comprehensive decomposition of valuation variation.
